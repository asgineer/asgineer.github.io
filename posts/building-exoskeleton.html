Building a Tendon-Driven Exoskeleton from Scratch

📅 October 12, 2023 ⏱ 12 min read ✏️ asgineer

When I started my master's thesis, I had a simple research question: can a tendon-driven, cable-based exoskeleton provide reliable assistive torque at the elbow joint for rehabilitation purposes? Two years later, I had a functional prototype and a headful of hard-earned lessons. This post walks through the entire journey — design philosophy, hardware, control, and testing.

Why Tendon-Driven?

Most rigid-link exoskeletons place motors directly on the joints, resulting in high inertia and rigid force transmission. Tendon-driven designs route flexible cables (tendons) from remote actuators to joints via Bowden conduits — similar to how bicycle brake cables work. This keeps motors away from the limb, reduces inertia, and makes the mechanism more comfortable to wear.

The downsides? Cable stretch, friction, and backlash introduce non-linearities that make precise force control significantly harder. That's where the interesting engineering lives.

Mechanical Design

The exoskeleton targets 4 DOF: elbow flexion/extension, forearm pronation/supination, wrist flexion/extension, and wrist deviation. The structural frame is 3D-printed PLA with aluminium insert brackets for the cable routing pulleys.

Key design constraints

  • Total weight on the arm ≤ 800 g
  • Maximum assistive torque: 6 N·m at the elbow
  • Range of motion: ≥ 120° for all joints
  • Safety: passive back-drivability + emergency release velcro straps

All parts were modelled in SolidWorks and validated with FEA before printing. The Bowden conduit anchors proved to be the weakest link in early versions — we went through three iterations before arriving at a Ø6 mm aluminium tube with a threaded M6 end insert.

Actuation System

Each DOF is driven by a pair of brushless DC motors (Maxon EC-i 40) paired with harmonic drive gearboxes (gear ratio 100:1). Motor current is measured in real time and used as a proxy for cable tension, eliminating the need for inline load cells at each joint. 

// Approximate cable force from motor current
F_cable = (K_t * I_motor * eta_gearbox) / r_spool

where K_t is the motor torque constant, eta_gearbox is the gearbox efficiency, and r_spool is the winch drum radius.

Control Architecture

The control loop runs at 1 kHz on a Beckhoff CX2040 IPC using TwinCAT 3. The outer impedance controller generates a desired joint torque based on deviation from a reference trajectory; the inner current controller executes this on each motor individually.

Impedance control

The impedance law is:

τ = K·(θ_d − θ) + B·(θ̇_d − θ̇)

where K and B are stiffness and damping gains, and θ_d is the desired trajectory. Gains were tuned with a Ziegler-Nichols approach on bench, then refined during patient trials.

Results & Lessons Learned

Five healthy subjects participated in a pilot study performing elbow flexion tasks under three conditions: no assistance, full assistance, and proportional myoelectric control. The exoskeleton successfully reduced measured EMG amplitude by ~38% in the full-assistance condition — consistent with the design target.

The biggest lessons:

  • Cable pre-tensioning is everything — slop kills performance.
  • Thermal management of the Maxon motors under sustained load was underestimated.
  • Real-time EtherCAT sync jitter must be less than 50 µs for stable force control.
  • Users vary wildly. Adjustability features that you think are unnecessary aren't.

Next Steps

The next iteration will replace the EtherCAT network with CAN FD to reduce wiring complexity, and integrate an sEMG interface for intent detection. I'm also exploring carbon fibre frame components to reduce the arm-side weight below 500 g.

Full source code (TwinCAT projects, MATLAB analysis scripts, and SolidWorks models) is available on GitHub.
